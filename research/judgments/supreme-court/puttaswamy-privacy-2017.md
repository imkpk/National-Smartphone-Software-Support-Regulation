## 6. Facts (neutral) [FACT]

Reference on whether privacy is a fundamental right under the Constitution, arising in the broader context of Aadhaar and related debates.

## 7. Issues [FACT/LAW]

Whether the right to privacy is protected as a fundamental right; its sources and contours under Part III.

## 8. Holding [LAW]

The Court held that privacy is a fundamental right protected under the Constitution, principally under Article 21 (and related freedoms), with a multi-opinion structure. Exact paragraph mapping PROVISIONAL pending full pin-cite verification.

## 9. Important observations [LAW/ANALYSIS]

Informational privacy, dignity, and autonomy themes; privacy not absolute—subject to lawful restrictions as developed in the opinions.

## 10. Legal principles established [LAW]

- Privacy as fundamental right
- Informational privacy recognised
- Limits via legality, legitimate aim, proportionality (as developed)

## 11. Relevance to this repository [ANALYSIS]

**Strength: High (digital rights / Art. 21).** Unpatched devices can threaten informational and financial privacy; central interpretive context for digital-personhood arguments. Does **not** impose OEM update-year duties.

**Not asserted:** That this judgment creates a legal duty requiring smartphone manufacturers to provide OS or security updates for any specified minimum period.

## 12. Limitations

Does not regulate product design or manufacturer software-support schedules. Application to private OEM conduct requires a State-action / regulatory theory (OPEN).

## 13. Open questions [OPEN]

2. What State duties arise when essential services run on personal smartphones?
